# Sticky Ideas

## Overview

Why do some ideas survive while others die? Not because they're more true — because they're better packaged. This skill applies the SUCCESs framework from Chip & Dan Heath's *Made to Stick* to make your ideas understood, remembered, and acted upon.

**Use alongside:** `clear-writing` for foundational clarity, `professional-communication` for format/channel, `copywriting` for marketing copy.

## When to Use

- Writing executive summaries, project pitches, or proposals
- Explaining technical decisions to non-technical stakeholders
- Creating product descriptions, landing page copy, or onboarding text
- Summarizing a complex system in a way people remember
- Writing PRDs, design docs, or strategy documents
- Any time your writing is correct but doesn't land

## The SUCCESs Framework

Six principles for making ideas stick. Use as a checklist — not every message needs all six, but the best ones use most.

### 1. Simple

**Find the core. Strip everything else.**

Not short — *core.* Proverbs are the ideal, not sound bites. "A bird in hand is worth two in the bush" is both simple AND profound.

| Instead Of | Try |
|-----------|-----|
| "Our platform leverages microservices architecture with event-driven communication to enable horizontal scaling" | "Each part of the system runs and scales independently" |
| "We need to optimize our customer acquisition funnel to reduce CAC and improve LTV ratios" | "We're spending too much to get customers who don't stay" |

**The Commander's Intent test:** If your team could only remember ONE thing from your message, what should it be? Lead with that.

**Technique — Generative analogy:** Anchor new ideas to something familiar. "It's like Uber, but for dog walkers" does more work than a paragraph of features. Good analogies let the audience generate new understanding on their own.

### 2. Unexpected

**Break a pattern, then fill the gap.**

Two parts:
1. **Get attention** — through surprise (violate expectations)
2. **Hold attention** — through curiosity (open a knowledge gap)

**Surprise works by:** Presenting information that contradicts what the audience expects. "Most startups don't fail from building the wrong thing — they fail from building the right thing too slowly."

**Curiosity works by:** Opening a gap between what people know and what they want to know. Pose a question or mystery before giving the answer.

| Approach | Example |
|----------|---------|
| Challenge assumption | "The most dangerous security vulnerability isn't in your code — it's in your npm dependencies" |
| Open a gap | "There are three reasons our deploy pipeline takes 40 minutes. The third one surprised us." |
| Counter-intuitive stat | "We reduced page load time by 60% by adding more JavaScript" |

**Anti-pattern:** Don't manufacture surprise. False cliffhangers or misleading hooks erode trust. The surprise must be *true*.

### 3. Concrete

**Speak in things people can see, touch, count.**

Abstract language sounds smart but doesn't stick. Concrete language creates mental images.

| Abstract (forgettable) | Concrete (sticky) |
|-----------------------|-------------------|
| "Improve system performance" | "Pages load in under 200ms" |
| "Enhance user experience" | "Users complete checkout in 3 taps" |
| "Significant cost savings" | "Saves $4,200/month on infrastructure" |
| "Robust error handling" | "The system retries failed payments 3 times, then emails the customer" |

**The Velcro Theory:** Concrete ideas have more "hooks" for memory to latch onto. "Bicycle" creates a mental image. "Vehicle" doesn't.

**Technique:** When explaining a system, walk through a specific example end-to-end rather than describing it abstractly. "When a user clicks 'Buy,' here's exactly what happens..." beats "The purchase flow handles transaction processing."

### 4. Credible

**Make it believable without relying on authority.**

Three credibility sources:

| Source | When to Use | Example |
|--------|------------|---------|
| **External authority** | You have hard data or expert backing | "According to our load tests..." |
| **Testable credential** | Let the audience verify themselves | "Try it — run the benchmark on your machine" |
| **Vivid detail** | You're telling a story | Specific numbers, names, and dates signal truthfulness |

**Anti-statistics:** A single vivid case study often persuades more than aggregate data. "One customer reduced their deploy time from 45 minutes to 3 minutes" hits harder than "average 80% improvement."

**The Sinatra Test:** "If I can make it there, I can make it anywhere." One undeniable proof point that makes everything else credible. If your system handles Black Friday traffic for Amazon, no one questions if it can handle their startup.

### 5. Emotional

**Make them care, not just understand.**

People act on feelings, not facts. Connect your idea to something they already care about.

**Techniques:**

- **Individual over statistics.** "One Nigerian SME owner lost 3 months of revenue to a single uninsured flood" moves people more than "40% of Nigerian SMEs lack adequate insurance."
- **Appeal to identity.** "People like us do things like this." Frame your idea as aligned with who the audience sees themselves as.
- **Appeal to self-interest.** Not just "what does this do?" but "what does this do for *you*?"
- **Move up Maslow's hierarchy.** Don't just sell features (base level). Sell belonging, mastery, purpose.

| Level | Approach | Example |
|-------|----------|---------|
| Features | "What it does" | "Automated deployments" |
| Benefits | "Why it helps you" | "Ship features faster with confidence" |
| Identity | "Who you become" | "Be the team that never has a deployment outage" |

### 6. Stories

**Wrap your idea in a narrative.**

Stories serve two functions:
1. **Simulation** — the audience mentally rehearses the scenario, learning how to act
2. **Inspiration** — the audience feels motivated to act

**Three universal plot patterns:**

| Plot | Structure | Use For |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Challenge** | Protagonist overcomes obstacle | Inspiring action against difficult odds |
| **Connection** | People bridge a gap (social, racial, expertise) | Building empathy and collaboration |
| **Creativity** | Someone solves a puzzle in an unexpected way | Encouraging innovative thinking |

**In technical writing:** Use the Challenge plot. "We were hitting 500 errors every deploy. Here's how we fixed it." is a story. It has a problem, a struggle, and a resolution. It teaches AND inspires.

**Anti-pattern:** Don't force stories. Some messages (error codes, API docs, changelogs) should be direct. Use stories for pitches, case studies, postmortems, and onboarding.

## Quick Stickiness Checklist

Before sending an important message, score it:

- [ ] **Simple** — Can I state the core in one sentence?
- [ ] **Unexpected** — Does it challenge an assumption or open a curiosity gap?
- [ ] **Concrete** — Are there specific numbers, examples, or images?
- [ ] **Credible** — Why should they believe me? Can they verify?
- [ ] **Emotional** — Will they *care*, not just understand?
- [ ] **Story** — Is there a narrative that makes the idea live?

**You don't need all six.** Even adding one or two principles to an otherwise forgettable message transforms it.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Burying the lead in context | State the core idea first (Simple) |
| All data, no feeling | Add one human example (Emotional + Concrete) |
| Abstract feature lists | Walk through a specific user scenario (Concrete + Story) |
| "Trust me" without evidence | Add one verifiable proof point (Credible) |
| Predictable structure that readers skim | Lead with a surprising fact or question (Unexpected) |
| Overloading with every detail | Cut to the core, defer the rest to appendix (Simple) |
